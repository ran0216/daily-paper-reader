## ReSAFT: An E ffi cient Stuck-at Fault-Tolerant Scheme for ReRAM-based Process-in-Memory Accelerators 

Aniseh Dorostkar, Hamed Farbeh ∗, Hamid R. Zarandi 

> Department of Computer Engineering, Amirkabir University of Technology (Tehran Polytechnic)

Abstract 

Analog ReRAM-based process-in-memory (PIM) accelerators provide high parallelism and energy e ffi ciency for deep convolu-tional neural networks (CNNs) inference. However, their susceptibility to permanent faults, such as stuck-at high (SaH ) and 

stuck-at low (SaL ) resistance states, poses a major challenge by permanently corrupting the CNN weights mapped to conductance values of ReRAM cells and degrading inference accuracy, which leads to system unreliability in safety-critical applications. In this paper, we propose a fault-tolerant scheme for analog ReRAM-based PIM accelerators to tackle stuck-at faults (SAFs) with minimal redundancy overhead to recover classification accuracy degradation. The proposed scheme contains a redundancy-based hardware solution alongside fault-aware mapping method for ensuring reliable analog computation in ReRAM crossbar. We analyze the impact of varying number of redundant rows and columns on accuracy and design metrics. Subsequently, a multi-objective opti-mization (MOO) problem is formulated and solved to e ffi ciently determine the number of redundant rows and columns, considering trade-o ff s among various design metrics. Furthermore, a fault-aware weight mapping is proposed for dual-crossbar structures to further compensate for the accuracy degradation caused by SAFs. Simulation results show that, for the SimpleNet model using the 

MNIST dataset, the inference accuracy is recovered by approximately 22.39%, on average, across four configurations of optimal solutions, each o ff ering a trade-o ff between reliability and area, energy consumption, and latency overheads. The mean-time-to-failure (MTTF) improves by about 61 × on average compared to the baseline. These selected configurations also reduce energy and area overheads by 32%, on average, in comparison to row-only and column-only configurations. 

Keywords: Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), fault-tolerant, hardware accelerators, process-in-memory (PIM), resistive random-access memory (ReRAM) 

1. Introduction 

Deep neural networks (DNNs), particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have greatly impacted applications such as healthcare, autonomous vehicles, space missions, and other safety-critical systems [1]. Their remarkable performance in classification, detection, and recognition comes from deep, multi-layered architectures that extract features through vector-matrix multiplication (VMM) operations. However, increas-ing network complexity to achieve higher accuracy also sig-nificantly raises computational cost, memory /storage require-ments, and energy consumption. To meet the demanding computational needs of CNNs, con-ventional computing platforms based on CPUs, graph pro-cessing units (GPUs) and field-programmable gate arrays (FP-GAs) are increasingly facing performance bottlenecks such as the memory wall and the power wall. Due to these limita-tions, processing-in-memory (PIM) paradigm have gained at-tention, which integrate computation directly within memory

Among various memory technologies enabling PIM, Re-sistive Random-Access Memory (ReRAM) has emerged as a promising candidate due to its non-volatility, high density, low power consumption, and capability to perform analog PIM. Us-ing the ReRAM crossbar structure, ReRAM-based PIM accel-erators such as ISAAC [2], PRIME [3], and PipeLayer [4] can perform massively memory-intensive VMM operations in par-allel, making them highly e ffi cient for CNN inference. Despite the mentioned advantages, due to the limitation of fabrication technology, ReRAM-based PIM accelerators suf-fer from process variations (PVs) [5, 6], limited write en-durance [6, 7], sources of noises [8], and nonlinearity of cur-rent–voltage characteristics [9] and hardware faults [9–12]. ReRAM faults are categorized into transient and permanent types based on their temporal characteristics [13], and arise either in fabrication phase or operational phase. Transient faults are temporary and non-destructive which typically caused by disturbances like high energy particle hitting the electri-cal device, resistance drift, PVs, temperature fluctuations, or read /write disturbance [1, 14, 15]. These faults change the re-sistance of the cells without physically damage the ReRAM cells as they can still function, because the resistance of the cell can change.

In contrast, permanent faults are persistent over time and the resistance of cell is unchangeable. These faults are due to irre-versible physical damage, often introduced by fabrication lim-itations (oxide layer thickness, process fluctuations, or reduced feature sizes [16, 17]) or limitation in endurance [7, 8]. Defects such as short defects and overforming stage defects [17], cause the resistance of a cell to stuck-at a resistance value ( stuck-at high (SaH ), stuck-at low (SaL ) or stuck-at value (SaV ) resistant state). SaH faults are commonly caused by short defects that force the cell to remain in high resistance state (HRS), while 

SaL faults may result from permanent cell failures, broken se-lectors, or damaged interconnects that force the cell to remain in low resistance state (LRS). Regardless of passing the man-ufacturing tests of ReRAM cells, permanent faults can occur over lifetime due to limited write endurance, excessive write-0 (or write-1) pulses [6, 18], or by damages to the device or transistor (e.g., due to wear out [19]). Since NN parameters are mapped to the conductance val-ues of ReRAM cells, any faults in ReRAM cells can easily corrupt these learned values, directly impact the outcomes of VMM, leading to computational inaccuracies. Consequently, process variations (PVs) and faults in ReRAM technology not only lower the yield of memory arrays but also impair the infer-ence accuracy of CNNs executed on PIM accelerators [20]. The vulnerability of ReRAM-based PIM accelerators in executing CNNs is highly sensitive to factors such as the type and depth of network layers, the location of learning parameters within each layer, as well as the value and types of the faults [21]. To address the reliability limitations of ReRAM-based PIM systems against faults, various software- and hardware-based fault-tolerant methods including mapping-based methods [14, 22–26], fault-aware training methods [5, 14, 16, 27, 28], error detection and correction codes [29–34], and redundancy-based methods [35], have been proposed. Although software-based methods are cost-e ff ective, most of them are not applicable to permanent faults due to the unchangeable resistance of faulty cells or become ine ffi cient under high fault rates. Conversely, existing hardware redundancy solutions, primar-ily designed for digital memory-centric applications, are not directly applicable to analog ReRAM-based PIM systems. In PIM crossbars, the fundamental computational unit is a col-umn performing VMM; consequently, a single faulty cell can compromise the functionality of the entire column. Although 1T1R structures allow faulty cells to be isolated by control-ling their associated access transistors [36]. this approach in-troduces substantial routing overhead. Furthermore, conven-tional redundancy-based methods typically rely solely on re-dundant rows or columns and deactivate an entire row or col-umn once a fault is detected. However, these methods are often too coarse-grained for analog computing, resulting in substan-tial ine ffi ciencies in area, power consumption, and latency that diminish the inherent energy and throughput advantages of PIM accelerators. Consequently, there is a critical need for a solution that bal-ances the trade-o ff between hardware cost, reliability, and in-ference accuracy in CNN models through integrated hardware-software-based solutions. Specifically, leveraging an optimal level of hardware and software redundancy mechanisms can ef-fectively compensate for accuracy loss caused by SAFs. This work is motivated by trading o ff the reliability, cost, and inference accuracy in analog ReRAM-based PIM accelera-tors. Our goal is to design a fault-tolerant architecture that tack-les SAFs with minimal redundancy overhead while preserving CNN classification accuracy. To this end, we propose ReSAFT, a fault-tolerant scheme for analog ReRAM-based PIM acceler-ators that combines a redundancy-based ReRAM crossbar ar-chitecture with fault-aware mapping and remapping methods to tolerate SAF e ffi ciently. The proposed fault-tolerant cross-bar architecture integrates a combination of redundant rows and columns into the ReRAM crossbar structure with signif-icantly fewer redundant resources compared to conventional redundancy schemes. We evaluate the impact of varying the number of redundant rows and columns on CNNs classification accuracy and design metrics, including reliability, energy con-sumption, area overhead, and latency. We then formulated and solved an architecture-level optimization problem to determine the adequate number of redundant rows and columns, a prob-lem that requires solving complex multi-dimensional trade-o ff samong these design metrics. To e ffi ciently utilize resources, we further propose software-level mapping and input schedul-ing across redundant rows and columns, and a dual-crossbar remapping method, which are applied at runtime. The main contributions of this article are as follows: 1. We propose a hardware redundancy-based fault-tolerant crossbar architecture for analog ReRAM-based PIM ac-celerators. Unlike conventional methods that rely solely on either row or column redundancy, the proposed ar-chitecture employs a combination of redundant rows and columns that uses the strengths of both to tolerate SaH and 

SaL faults Specifically, redundant columns are used to tol-erate both SaL and SaH faults, while redundant rows toler-ate SaH faults via current adjustment, significantly reduc-ing area overhead compared to column-only methods. 2. A multi-objective optimization (MOO) problem is formu-lated and solved to determine the adequate number of re-dundant rows and columns in ReRAM-based crossbars. The formulation considers trade-o ff s among design met-rics including MTTF, energy consumption, latency, and area while satisfying a classification accuracy constraint. 3. To e ffi ciently use the hardware redundancy, a fault-aware mapping algorithm is proposed to su ffi ciently map faulty cells to appropriate redundant cells based on the specific fault type and location. This is an algorithmic enhance-ment to the PIM controller that works with the proposed redundant-based crossbar. 4. A complementary and low-cost fault-aware weight remap-ping is applied at runtime across dual ReRAM crossbars (contain positive and negative crossbars) for the remain-ing faults that cannot be tolerated by ReFTC architecture. 2.3. Related Work 

Several studies have investigated the reliability and vulner-ability of CNNs to both permanent and transient faults across a variety of hardware platforms, including CPUs, GPUs, ten-sor processing units (TPUs), FPGAs, application-specific in-tegrated circuits (ASICs) [1, 13, 38], and ReRAM-based PIM accelerators [21, 23, 39]. To recover the degraded accuracy of CNNs caused by faults in ReRAM-based accelerators, several software- and hardware-based methods have been proposed. Software-based methods typically focus on algorithmic strategies to mitigate the impact of both transient faults (e.g., re-sistance drift and process variation) and permanent faults. No-table methods include parameter mapping or remapping [22– 26], retraining [5, 12, 22, 25, 36, 40], fault- and variation-aware online training [5, 14, 16, 27, 28], and matrix transformation methods, e.g., row flipping, matrix permutation and value range transformation [41], all of which have been proposed to im-prove the reliability of the inference phase in NNs. While these methods can improve fault tolerance without modifying the hardware architecture, they often introduce additional com-putational cost or memory overhead. In particular, retraining and online training approaches require frequent weight updates, resulting in a large number of write operations in ReRAM de-vices. Prior studies report that the number of training iterations for on-device training can reach on the order of 10 4 −10 8, while the average endurance of a memristor cell is about 10 10 write cycles [20], potentially accelerating device wear-out. More-over, the e ff ectiveness of software-based fault-tolerant methods significantly degrades under high fault rates and large device variations. Prior study reports that when the fault rate exceeds approximately 10%-15% of crossbar cells, the classification ac-curacy of CNN models may drop by more than 20%–40% with remapping methods [42]. To specifically address faults in analog vector–matrix mul-tiplication, several architecture-aware approaches have been proposed. For instance, a row shu ffl ing method is introduced in [43], followed by post-processing computations to compen-sate the output errors. Several studies exploit the di ff erential property of the hardware structure (the presence of two cur-rent paths used to generate signed weights, such as in dual-crossbar structure with positive and negative crossbars or dif-ferential column structures) as an opportunity for fault com-pensation without requiring additional hardware [20, 23, 42]. In [23], a fault-aware weight decomposition (FAWD) method is proposed to mitigate the impact of stuck-at faults (SAFs) in binary ReRAM crossbars, while [42] extends this approach to analog ReRAM crossbars. In addition, the forward parameter tuning (FPT) scheme proposed in [44] exploits the statistical properties of DNN computations to update network parameters in binary ReRAM devices. Although these approaches can re-duce accuracy degradation caused by device-level faults, they often introduce additional computational overhead or require iterative parameter updates. In contrast, hardware-based methods improve system relia-bility by introducing redundancy or modifying peripheral cir-cuits [16, 35, 45]. A common approach is the use of er-ror detection and correction codes (ECC) [29–34], which em-ploy additional encoder and decoder circuits to detect and cor-rect errors. These circuits introduce extra hardware complex-ity and may result in approximately 15%-40% additional area and power overhead, depending on the coding scheme and im-plementation, while improve accuracy about 50%-60%. Re-dundancy can also be implemented through additional ReRAM cells [35], redundant rows or columns, or redundant computa-tion modules. For example, triple modular redundancy (TMR) replicates hardware modules three times and determines the final output using majority voting [45], which increases the hardware cost by nearly 200% compared with the baseline design. Other redundancy-based methods employ redundant columns or crossbars to replace faulty ReRAM columns [35]. A distribution-aware redundancy scheme was proposed in [35], which utilizes redundant ReRAM columns or crossbars to toler-ate SAFs, while redundancy ratios ranging from approximately 200% and 40% to 60% additional cells. However, applying redundancy in analog PIM accelerators is more challenging than in conventional memory designs. Consequently, conven-tional redundancy approaches, such as disabling entire rows or columns, may be too coarse-grained for analog computation and can lead to ine ffi ciencies in area, energy consumption, and latency. Hardware compensation methods have also been pro-posed, such as modified transimpedance amplifier (TIA) cir-cuits to compensate for SAF-induced error currents in ReRAM crossbar arrays [35, 46]. Despite extensive research on fault-tolerant techniques, most existing solutions are either designed for digital ReRAM cross-bars or focus on memory-oriented applications. These ap-proaches may not be directly suitable for analog ReRAM-based PIM accelerators that require accurate analog VMMs. Further-more, purely algorithm-level methods often become less ef-fective under high fault rates or large device variations, while 4hardware-based redundancy inevitably introduces additional hardware cost and power consumption. Therefore, combining hardware redundancy with software-based methods can provide a more balanced trade-o ff among reliability, hardware over-head, and classification accuracy. In this work, we investigate the impact of redundancy ratio on classification accuracy, mean time to failure (MTTF), en-ergy consumption, and area overhead in ReRAM-based PIM accelerators. We further optimize redundancy allocation to en-hance the reliability of ReRAM crossbars in the presence of SAFs. In addition, a fault-aware mapping algorithm is proposed to further reduce the accuracy loss caused by SAFs. Compared with prior studies, the proposed scheme jointly considers redun-dancy design and fault-aware weight remapping, enabling more effi cient and reliable computation in analog ReRAM-based ac-celerators. While prior work proposes valuable contributions, none provides a similar level of integration and e ffi ciency in tolerating SAFs in analog ReRAM-based accelerators. 

3. ReSAFT: Fault-Tolerant Scheme for Analog ReRAM-Based PIM Accelerator 

The proposed Stuck-At Fault-Tolerant scheme for ReRAM crossbar, called ReSAFT , proposes a hardware- software-based solutions to tolerate SAFs occurs in ReRAM crossbars that performs analog VMMs for CNNs. ReSAFT utilize a redundancy-based architecture with runtime fault-aware map-ping and remapping method to tolerate SaH and SaL faults e ffi -ciently. The overall workflow of the proposed ReSAFT is illus-trated in Fig. 4. The redundancy-based fault-tolerant ReRAM crossbar ( ReFTC ) architecture integrates a combination of re-dundant rows and columns into the ReRAM crossbar struc-ture to tolerate SAFs with minimal redundancy overhead. Un-like traditional methods that allocate redundancy heuristically, the ReFTC solution comprises two main stages: 1) employ-ing a Multi-Objective Optimization (MOO) approach at design time to determine an adequate number of redundant rows and columns by considering reliability, MTTF, energy consump-tion, latency, area, and the classification accuracy of CNNs, and 2) Once the hardware configuration is fabricated and the specific fault map of each crossbar is identified, a mapping and scheduling algorithm (called RMap) is applied at runtime to ef-ficiently utilize the available redundant rows and columns. The RMap maps faulty cells between redundant rows and columns and schedules the corresponding input voltages for redundant rows accordingly. To further enhance the fault tolerance of the accelerator, a Fault-Aware Dual crossbar Mapping ( FADM ) method is em-ployed at runtime to remap the conductance value of faulty cells to their corresponding dual cells in the positive or nega-tive ReRAM crossbars. we apply FADM specifically as a com-plementary and low-cost remapping method for the remaining faults that are not tolerated by the ReFTC architecture, as well as faults occurring in redundant rows and columns. 3.1. ReFTC: Redundancy-Based Fault-Tolerant ReRAM Cross-bar Architecture 

The proposed redundancy-based fault-tolerant ReRAM crossbar architecture enhances the fault tolerance of ReRAM-based PIM accelerators against SaH and SaL faults by incor-porating redundant rows and columns into the ReRAM cross-bar architecture. The resulting design, referred to as the redundancy-based fault-tolerant ReRAM crossbar ( ReFTC ), ar-chitecture is illustrated in Fig. 5. As depicted in the figure, the conventional 1T1R structure is employed as the basic cell for both the main crossbar and the redundant rows and columns. To tolerate faulty cells within the main crossbar, the architecture includes r redundant rows and c redundant columns. Further-more, r × c auxiliary redundant cells are placed at the intersec-tions of the redundant rows and redundant columns (as shown in Fig. 5) to tolerate possible faults occurring in the redundant columns themselves. In the proposed ReFTC architecture, a combination of redun-dant rows and columns is employed to enhance fault tolerance. If only redundant columns are used in the architecture, a re-dundant column is required for each faulty column in the main crossbar. However, when multiple faults occur within a sin-gle column, all of them can be tolerated using just one redun-dant column. Conversely, if multiple faults occur in a single row of the main crossbar, multiple redundant columns would be needed to tolerate these faults. while, the same faults can be handled using just one redundant row. A similar principle ap-plies when only redundant rows are utilized. If a high number of faults occur in a single column of the main crossbar, multiple redundant rows would be necessary, whereas a single redun-dant column would su ffi ce. Therefore, our fault-tolerant archi-tecture utilizing a combination of redundant rows and columns can achieve higher e ffi ciency compared to using only redundant rows or columns. Additionally, the redundant row, which can perform analog VMMs, a higher number of faults occurring in di ff erent rows of the main crossbar can be tolerated by introducing additional cy-cles with fewer additional rows. However, this method incurs a time overhead. For example, if five faults occur in various rows and columns of the main crossbar, all the faults can be tolerated for within five additional cycles using only one addi-tional row. 3.1.1. Mapping and Scheduling of Faulty Cells to Redundant Rows and Columns 

Prior to the mapping stage, a fault detection procedure is ap-plied to the entire crossbar, including the redundant rows, re-dundant columns, and auxiliary cells, using algorithms such as March-C [7, 47, 48] and Squeeze-search [7] to generate a fault map. If faults are detected in the crossbar, the proposed map-ping method, called RMap , is applied to map faulty cells of the main crossbar to the available redundant rows or columns, as well as to map faults in redundant columns to the available auxiliary redundant cells. Using redundant columns, both SaH 

and SaL faults occurring in a column of the main crossbar can be tolerated. However, SaL faults occurring in the main cross-bar or redundant columns cannot be tolerated using redundant rows, since a large current flows through the faulty cell and can-not be mitigated by the redundant rows. Therefore, redundant columns are prioritized for handling SaL faults during mapping. During the mapping stage, the proposed RMap algorithm considers not only faulty cells in the main crossbar but also faults occurring in the redundancy resources. If a fault is de-tected in a cell of a redundant column, the appropriate auxil-iary cell is used as a replacement. Faults occurring in redun-dant rows can often be tolerated through the proposed mapping and scheduling algorithm across the remaining redundant rows. Auxiliary cells are primarily used when a faulty cell appears in the redundancy structure and cannot be handled through remap-ping. When auxiliary cells are not used for replacement, they are programmed to the minimum conductance value ( gmin ) to avoid contributing current during VMM operations. Moreover, a recent study of ReRAM crossbars reported that 9.04% of the cells exhibit SaH resistance states, whereas 1.75% exhibit SaL resistance states [17]. This finding indicates that SaH faults are more prevalent in ReRAM cross-bar arrays compared to SaL faults. Accordingly, the proposed ReFTC architecture prioritizes the use of redundant columns for tolerating columns containing SaL faults, followed by columns with a high density of SaH and SaL faults, while redundant rows are primarily utilized for tolerating distributed SaH faults. If the number of available redundant rows and columns is insu ffi cient to tolerate all detected faults, the remaining untolerated faults are further mitigated using the proposed FADM method. Since the redundant resources in ReFTC are limited, the proposed framework provides bounded fault tolerance depending on the allocated redundancy budget determined during the design-time optimization stage. Consequently, the e ff ectiveness of fault tol-erance depends on both the available redundancy resources and the remapping capability of FADM. The proposed RMap method follows several steps to map faulty cells to redundant rows and columns: 1. If the number of faulty cells within a specific column (row) of the main crossbar surpasses a predefined threshold, the conductance values of that entire column are mapped to a redundant column (row), and the original faulty column (row) is deactivated. 2. While there are unused redundant columns, columns with SaL faults are deactivated and mapped to redundant columns. 3. In cases where the fault count does not exceed the thresh-old, SaH faulty cells are instead mapped to redundant rows. 4. SaH faulty cells are mapped to the same column in redun-dant rows as their original positions in the main crossbar, ensuring the index alignment is preserved. 65. Columns of redundant rows that contain no faulty cells are deactivated, and their cells are programmed to the mini-mum conductance value, gmin .It is important to note that when the number of redundant rows is less than the number of rows in the main crossbar, faults from di ff erent rows of the main crossbar may be mapped to a single redundant row. In this scenario, di ff erent input voltage values must be applied to that row in successive cycles to ensure accurate computations, which causes serialization overhead. To mitigate the latency overhead of serialization, the RMap pri-oritizes redundant columns for mapping (Steps 1 and 2), and the algorithm maps only the remaining, distributed faults to re-dundant rows. To e ffi ciently manage the mapping of remaining faults in redundant rows, we propose a mapping and schedul-ing strategy that determines which input should be applied to each row during each cycle and which columns of the redundant rows should be activated. The proposed scheduling strategy is given in Algorithm 1. This algorithm is designed to map and schedule SaH faults from main crossbar rows to the available redundant rows. It begins by sorting the main crossbar rows in descending order based on the number of SaH faults, obtaining both the sorted fault counts and their corresponding indices. Then, while there are still unmapped SaH faults, the algorithm iteratively selects a main-crossbar row x, which has the highest number of un-mapped faults (and is non-zero), and assigns it to cycle col for the current redundant row r. This value of x serves as the in-put index for the current cycle (line 9). It then attempts to map faults from row x to the redundant row r (line 10 to 12). The input scheduling for each cycle is recorded in a list called cy-cle col , which is later added to the overall cycle list to track the input sequence for each redundant row. If there are remaining faults and only a few rows left, a final mapping cycle is per-formed to ensure all faults are addressed (lines 14 to 17). The output includes: 1) redundant row , a list of size r × m that indi-cates the mapping of SaH faults in redundant rows and 2) cycle, a list that indicates the sequence of inputs that should be applied to each row in each cycle. 3.1.2. Determining Number of Redundant Rows and Columns 

The relationship between temporal and spatial redundancies as well as the number of rows and columns, involves a trade-o ff 

that should be carefully analyzed and optimized to balance the effi ciency and accuracy. The goal is to select the number of re-dundant rows and columns that minimize energy consumption, latency, and area, while maximizing the MTTF. At the same 7time, the degradation in CNN accuracy due to faults should re-main within a given threshold. This section presents a com-prehensive analysis and model of how varying the number of redundant rows and columns a ff ects reliability, MTTF, energy consumption, latency, area, and CNN classification accuracy. Based on this analysis, an optimization algorithm is proposed to determine the optimal redundancy configuration. 

3.1.2.1 Reliability of ReRAM Crossbar

If fault occurs, a redundant column re-places an entire faulty column. However, if the mapping in-volves a redundant row, only the faulty cell is mapped to one of the redundant cells in the same column as the location in the main crossbar, allowing both rows to remain active simultane-ously. 3.1.2.5 Accuracy 

Top-1 accuracy measures the proportion of correct predictions and is a proper metric to evaluate the impact of faults on CNNs and the e ff ectiveness of the ReSAFT scheme under faulty con-ditions. Exhaustively evaluating the accuracy of CNNs under all pos-sible combinations of redundant rows and columns is time and resource expensive. For example, for a 256 × 256 main cross-bar, considering 257 possible values (ranging from 0 to 256) for each redundancy types leads to over 65 , 000 configurations. Computing the accuracy of CNNs for all these combinations is not practical, especially for deeper networks or larger cross-bars. To address this challenge, we first evaluated the classifi-cation accuracy of CNNs under varying numbers of redundant rows and columns separately, and fitted two independent mod-els, Accuracy row (r) and Accuracy col (c), respectively. This ap-proach required only 514 simulations in total (257 for rows and 257 for columns), in contrast to over 65 , 000 simulations needed for all possible combinations. However, these one-dimensional models cannot directly estimate the accuracy for arbitrary ( r, c)combinations. Therefore, we propose an approximation model in which the total redundancy r + c is used as the input to both functions, and their outputs are averaged as: Accuracy FT (r, c) = Accuracy row (r + c) + Accuracy col (r + c)2 (20) This approach provides a good balance between accuracy and computational e ffi ciency, reducing the need for exhaustive simulations while maintaining acceptable estimation error. 10 3.1.3. 3.2. FADM: Fault-Aware Weight Mapping in Dual ReRAM Crossbar 

In the dual-crossbar architecture, which is originally em-ployed to store positive and negative weight values, faulty ReRAM cells can also be tolerated using their corresponding cells in the opposite crossbar. Since two types of faults ( SaH 

and SaL ) may occur in both positive and negative crossbars, each crossbar can be in one of three states: fault-free, SaH ,or SaL . Therefore, a pair of dual crossbars can experience nine possible fault cases. Among these cases, one corresponds to the fault-free condition, four cases occur when faults a ff ect a cell in both crossbars simultaneously (representing double faults) and are therefore intolerable. 4. Evaluation and Analysis 

4.1. Experiment Setup 

To evaluate the performance of our proposed methods, we conducted a set of experiments using well-known CNN models, including SimpleNet [51], LeNet5 [52], VGG8 [53], 

VGG11 [53], and ResNet18 [54]. SimpleNet and LeNet5 were trained on the MNIST dataset, which consists of handwritten digits, while VGG8 and ResNet18 were trained on the CIFAR-10 [55] dataset, a collection of small, color images. Both 

MNIST [52] and CIFAR-10 datasets contain 60,000 images for training and 10,000 images for testing, classified into ten di ff er-ent classes. The specifications of the CNN models evaluated in this study are presented in Table 2. PyTorch with the MemTorch 

library are used to model ReRAM-based DNNs and evaluate classification accuracy of faulty and non-faulty crossbars. All ReRAM devices were simulated using the VTEAM model [56], with RON = 1.4 × 10 4 and ROFF = 1.4 × 10 7.To estimate metrics such as accuracy, energy consump-tion, latency, and area utilization, the ReRAM-based PIM ar-chitecture was built using MNSIM2.0 [50], an open-source behavioral-level modeling tool for memristor-based PIM archi-tectures. We modified the crossbar architectures and add re-dundant rows /columns and peripheral circuit. For the ReRAM-based crossbar array (RCA) configuration, we adopted a 256 ×

256 dual-crossbar tile structure. This configuration includes positive and negative crossbars to store the positive and neg-ative CNN learning parameters, respectively. The RCA was implemented using a 1T1R structure with redundant rows and columns, as well as CMOS-based peripheral circuits, fabricated using TSMC 45nm device technology. Moreover, to solve the proposed MOO problem, we use the Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm-II (NSGA-II) [57] algorithm, and its implementation was conducted us-ing the Python Multi-Objective Optimization (PYMOO) frame-work [58], a dedicated Python library for multi-objective opti-mization. PYMOO provides a comprehensive set of tools for analyzing and managing trade-o ff s among competing objec-tives.
