Abstract 

We present a 28nm digital Ising accelerator with 27,648 spins across four chips. A time-multiplexed spin-update array with local SRAM and scheduled interchip transfers delivers 41.5G updates/s at 1.2pJ/update. Degree-15 Pegasus connectivity and 10b coefficients increase native connectivity and precision over degree-8, 5b multichip annealers. Programmable couplings sup-port optimization and probabilistic logic, with a four-chip planted-MaxCut trace reaching the solution for 27,069 nodes in 3.3 μs. 

Combinatorial optimization problems (COPs) arise in graph partitioning, routing, resource al-location, scheduling, and circuit design. Many COPs and Boolean satisfiability problems can be expressed as Ising models, motivating programmable accelerators that search for low-energy spin configurations [1]. Their hardware cost is set by both the number of spins and the connections between them. Fully connected digital accelerators support arbitrary pairwise interactions, but require O(N 2) coefficient storage and an O(N )-term weighted sum per spin for an N -spin prob-lem [2,3]. Sparse accelerators bound the number of neighbors, reducing coefficient storage to O(N )

and allowing independent spins to update in parallel [4, 5]. However, embedding a problem onto a sparse graph introduces additional physical spins to represent missing connections, limiting the logical problem size that fits on one chip [3]. Multichip integration increases this capacity, but dependent updates must now exchange spin states across chips. Prior two- and nine-chip CMOS annealers use degree-8 King’s graphs [6, 7]; the latter supports 5b coefficients and adapts spin-data compression during annealing to accommodate interchip latency. An 8K-spin accelerator supports up to 31 two- and three-body interactions through reconfigurable spin groups connected along a chip chain [8]. Scaling sparse accelerators therefore requires co-designing graph connectivity, local storage, and interchip transfer. This work uses degree-15 Pegasus connectivity to improve embedding efficiency while retaining the bounded storage and arithmetic cost of a sparse graph (Fig. 1). Developed for quantum anneal-ing, Pegasus adds connections to the degree-6 Chimera graph, providing fifteen neighbors per spin without full connectivity [9, 10]. In the illustrated embedding of a 32-spin fully connected problem, Pegasus requires 133 physical spins versus 1,885 on a King’s graph. Programmable 10b couplings and biases support general-purpose Ising and quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) mappings within the available spin capacity and coefficient precision. Different problem graphs are supported through coefficient programming and embedding, without application-specific datapaths. The 28nm digital accelerator implements this graph with three circuit and architecture tech-niques: (1) local coefficient SRAM feeding parallel probabilistic spin-update circuits, with no multi-pliers or per-coupling destination addresses; (2) a time-multiplexed processing-element (PE) array that stores 6,912 spins per chip and schedules independent updates in four color groups; and (3) chip-to-chip (C2C) links that exchange fixed-length boundary-state packets while the PEs process local spins. The update order creates a transfer interval before remote states are needed, allowing 1arXiv:2609.07907v1 [cs.AR] 7 Sep 2026 computation and communication to overlap. Four chips support 27,648 physical spins at 41.5G up-dates/s and 1.2pJ/update. Five representative workloads, spanning optimization and probabilistic logic, evaluate both programmability and operation on the coupled multichip graph. The four-color Pegasus partition groups spins that are not directly coupled. Updating one color at a time exposes parallelism within each group while preserving the dependencies of block Gibbs sampling [10], including interchip couplings. Fig. 2 shows the chip architecture: nine Ising units (IUs) in a 3 × 3 array, each integrating six PEs, coefficient SRAM, and spin-state registers. The 54 PEs update one color in parallel, and four sequential color updates produce 216 spin updates per global-clock cycle. To increase spin capacity without replicating the arithmetic circuits, the array processes 32 positions arranged as an 8 × 4

grid (Fig. 3). Each position contains nine 24-spin Pegasus cells, giving 32 × 9 × 24 = 6 ,912 stored spins per chip. A shared 7b address, comprising 5b position and 2b color indices, selects the SRAM word, neighbor states, and writeback registers. Each PE serves 128 stored spins. One pass through all position/color addresses completes a Monte Carlo sweep, updating every spin once. The SRAM organization matches the operand bandwidth of the PE array. Each spin requires a 160b record containing fifteen signed 10b coupling coefficients and one signed 10b bias. Within an IU, four 128 × 240 b single-port banks supply six records in parallel as a 960b word. Across nine IUs, the 36 banks store 1,105,920b of coefficients and biases; spin-state registers and communication buffers are separate. Fixed Pegasus connections select neighbor registers without per-coefficient addresses; zero coefficients disable unused connections. The memory-access sequence is unchanged across problems, and the per-IU SRAM bandwidth is independent of chip count. Each PE implements the weighted sum with sign-selection logic and an adder tree (Fig. 2). Since 

sj is binary, Jij sj selects either Jij or its negation, eliminating general-purpose multipliers. The tree accumulates fifteen coupling terms and the bias, after which a clamp circuit limits the input to a hyperbolic-tangent lookup table (LUT). A 7b magnitude address selects the LUT entry, while the input sign controls the comparison polarity. Comparing the LUT output with a linear-feedback shift register (LFSR) word implements P (si = +1) = [1 + tanh (βI i)]/2 . The sampled bit is written to the state register selected by the shared address. The host applies temperature scaling to the programmed coefficients and biases, so the PE requires no separate temperature multiplier. The C2C interface supplies neighbor states residing on an adjacent chip. Received bits are stored in boundary registers and selected through the same input multiplexers as on-chip states. Coupling coefficients remain in the destination SRAM; only 1b spin states cross the interface. To hide their transfer latency, the position sequence in Fig. 3 separates boundary-state generation from the dependent updates by half of the 32-position sweep. The PEs process intervening positions while the interface transmits these states. Each chip repeats this exchange without increasing PE fan-in, SRAM word width, or packet length. The packetizer exploits the fixed boundary connections to avoid address overhead (Fig. 2). Three edge IUs each contribute 14 north-boundary or 18 south-boundary states, forming 42b and 54b payloads, respectively. The serializer uses 6b north and 7b south data paths, with padding to complete each packet in eight I/O cycles; a shared 7b circuit accommodates both formats. At the receiver, the fixed bit order maps the reconstructed payload directly to boundary registers. Unlike 2activity-dependent compression, this fixed-length exchange transfers all required boundary states at every annealing stage. Source-synchronous signaling and asynchronous first-in, first-out (FIFO) buffers decouple the I/O and compute clocks. The transmitter forwards its I/O clock with the serialized data. A 32 × 7bFIFO at each endpoint uses binary read/write pointers for local addressing and synchronized Gray-coded pointers for full/empty detection. Ready/valid handshaking prevents overflow and underflow. The controller checks packet availability before executing a dependent update; if a packet is late, it holds the position/color address and disables spin writeback. This backpressure preserves update order, while transfers completed within the scheduled interval overlap computation without stalling the array. Coefficients and LFSR seeds are loaded through the scan interface, which also reads captured spin states. The four-chip prototype is evaluated on planted-MaxCut, spin-glass, frustrated-loop, semiprime-factorization, and 3SAT workloads. Fig. 4 shows a planted-MaxCut instance with 27,069 logical nodes and couplings spanning the chip boundaries. The planted solution forms an “IEEE” pat-tern across the four chips, with ground-state energy −13 ,824 . The displayed convergence trace reaches this state in 3.3 μs of compute time, exercising the interchip couplings during the search. Reprogramming the couplings for spin-glass instances produces the sampled-energy distributions and ASIC/CPU convergence traces in the same figure. ASIC samples concentrate at lower energies in the displayed distribution, while both implementations approach similar energies over the longer trace. The 16-spin energy landscape is a separate illustration of competing minima. Fig. 5 evaluates frustrated-loop optimization over planted-loop densities α of 0.5 to 8, where α

is the number of loops per p-bit. The reported time to solution at 99% success probability, TTS 99 ,targets an energy within 2% of the known ground state and counts ASIC-active time at 10MHz. In a separate four-chip experiment at α = 8 , two of sixteen runs reach the exact ground state, at 2.9s and 8.9s. Probabilistic logic is implemented by programming the couplings and biases of the corresponding logic cells, reusing the same PE circuit and update schedule. For semiprime factorization, the output distributions peak at 13 and 11 for the target product 143. For Boolean satisfiability with three literals per clause (3SAT), the plotted TTS 99 ranges from approximately 0.8 to 1.3s for 200 to 1,200 clauses, with twelve samples per point. Fig. 6 compares the accelerator with digital CMOS Ising implementations spanning different graph structures and coefficient precisions [8, 12–14, 16]. Sparse SRAM macros and reconfigurable PE arrays trade connectivity against storage and routing cost [11, 12, 15]. Relative to the nine-chip King’s-graph annealer [7], this design increases native degree from 8 to 15 and coefficient precision from 5b to 10b while retaining O(N ) storage. At a 48MHz global clock and 0.9V core supply, with 24MHz I/O at 1.2V, the four-chip array supports 216 × 4 × 48 M updates/s, or 41.5G updates/s. This is the aggregate update-attempt rate, including updates that retain the previous state, rather than a workload-dependent solution rate. The reported 1.2pJ/update corresponds to 49.8mW at this aggregate rate. Fabricated in 28nm HPC+ CMOS, each chip occupies approximately 1.5mm 2, with a 1.203 ×

0.703 mm 2 core (0.846mm 2) and 0.299mm 2 of coefficient SRAM (Fig. 7). The core, I/O pad ring, and pad-to-core channel account for 56%, 33%, and 11% of die area, respectively. A field-programmable gate array (FPGA) on the test board configures the chips and captures spin states. Together, Pegasus embedding, local SRAM, and scheduled C2C transfers support parallel spin updates on a programmable 27,648-spin multichip graph. 3References
